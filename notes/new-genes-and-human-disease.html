Rare diseases and new genes in human evolution

What Mendelian disease phenotypes reveal about how young genes shape human evolution.

As an evolutionary geneticist, I have always been curious about a seemingly simple question: why are rare diseases so rare?

This project did not start with me. It began with Dr. Patrick Landback, the second author of our paper and a former graduate student of Dr. Manyuan Long at the University of Chicago, who now works in fisheries in Alaska (probably, according to Manyuan—I have not verified this with the fish).

Patrick originally developed this project with Manyuan, focusing on the role of new genes in rare diseases. The path to publication, however, was not straightforward. I first heard about the project in 2017, when I was a visiting student at the University of Chicago and Manyuan told me about the idea. At the time, I knew very little about human disease genetics. Several years later, after Patrick had graduated, Manyuan asked me to take over the project.

In 2019, I joined West China Hospital of Sichuan University and continued working on the project remotely under Manyuan’s mentorship. Our goal was simple: to develop the original idea further and eventually bring the work to publication.

As the analysis progressed, one pattern kept emerging: evolutionarily new genes were relatively rare among rare-disease genes.

At first, this seemed like a weakness in the story. But through conversations with physicians, reading the literature, and participating in rare-disease genetic analyses myself, I gradually came to see this pattern as both reasonable and biologically meaningful.

Rare-disease diagnosis demands precision. Among many candidate genes, evolutionarily conserved genes are often easier to prioritize as potentially causal, and their functions can also be more readily investigated in model organisms such as mice. New genes, by contrast, tend to have narrower expression patterns—many are expressed in only a few tissues, particularly the testis—and may therefore be less likely to produce the broad or recognizable phenotypes typically associated with Mendelian disorders.

What we did

We combined gene-age estimates from GenTree with Mendelian disease phenotypes from the Human Phenotype Ontology. Across approximately 19,665 human genes, nearly 5,000 disease-associated genes could be placed along an evolutionary timeline, ranging from ancient genes shared across vertebrates to evolutionarily young genes specific to primates or humans.

We then integrated de novo germline variants from more than 46,000 parent-offspring trios with population variation from gnomAD to examine mutational burden and selective constraint across genes of different evolutionary ages.

What we found

The proportion of genes associated with Mendelian disease increases progressively with gene age. Older genes tend to be longer and carry a greater burden of deleterious de novo variants.

At the same time, evolutionarily new genes associated with biomedical phenotypes appear to have been incorporated into the human genome at a remarkably steady rate—approximately 0.07% per million years.

Young genes are particularly enriched for diseases affecting the male reproductive system. The X chromosome also carries an excess of genes associated with male reproductive phenotypes, a pattern consistent with the evolutionary forces underlying the faster-X and faster-male effects and with a potential role for sexual selection.

Young genes are also associated with phenotypes related to evolutionary innovations in humans, including brain size, intellectual development, musculoskeletal traits, and color vision.

Perhaps most interestingly, we found that pleiotropy increases with gene age following a logistic trajectory. This observation led us to propose a “pleiotropy-barrier” model: young genes initially have relatively few functions and therefore face fewer functional constraints, leaving greater evolutionary space for phenotypic innovation. As genes become older and accumulate additional functions, increasing pleiotropy may progressively constrain their evolutionary trajectories.

Why are rare diseases rare?

Looking back, the answer to my original question may lie partly in the evolutionary histories of the genes themselves.

Genes underlying Mendelian diseases tend to be evolutionarily old and conserved. Their conservation reflects, in part, strong purifying selection: damaging variants affecting important and highly constrained genes are efficiently removed from populations. This evolutionary constraint may help explain why many severe Mendelian disorders—and the variants that cause them—remain rare.

In this sense, rare diseases offer more than a window into human pathology. They also provide a record of how genes acquire functions, accumulate constraints, and contribute to phenotypic innovation over evolutionary time.
