New genes and human disease

What Mendelian disease phenotypes reveal about how young genes shape human evolution.

As an evolutionary geneticist, I have always been curious about why rare diseases are so rare.

This project did not start with me. It began with Dr. Patrick Landback, the second author of the paper and a former graduate student of Dr. Manyuan Long at the University of Chicago, who now works in fisheries in Alaska (probably, according to Manyuan; I have not verified this with the fish). Patrick first wanted to work with Manyuan on this topic, and his early manuscript focused mainly on the role of new genes in rare diseases. The submission process did not go smoothly. I first heard about the idea in 2017, when I was a visiting student at the University of Chicago and Manyuan told me about it. At that time I knew little about disease genetics. Years after Patrick graduated, Manyuan asked me to take over the work.

In 2019 I began working at West China Hospital, Sichuan University, and continued the project remotely under Manyuan's mentorship. We kept working on it together, with the goal of bringing it to publication. As the analysis went on, I kept seeing the same pattern: new genes were relatively rare among rare disease genes.

At first this looked like a weakness of the story. But through conversations with physicians I know, reading the literature, and taking part in rare disease analyses myself, I came to see it as a reasonable and meaningful finding. Rare disease diagnosis aims for precision. Among many candidate genes, the more conserved a gene is, the more likely it is to be considered causal, and conserved genes are also easier to test in mice. New genes, in contrast, are often expressed in only a few tissues, especially the testis, so they may not produce the kinds of phenotypes that are recognized as rare diseases.

What we did

We combined gene age dating from GenTree with Mendelian disease phenotypes from the Human Phenotype Ontology. Across about 19,665 human genes, nearly 5,000 disease genes could be placed on the evolutionary timeline, from genes shared with bony vertebrates to genes that are specific to primates and humans. We then brought in de novo germline variants from more than 46,000 trios and population variation from gnomAD to look at mutation burden and selective constraint.

What we found

  • The fraction of disease genes rises gradually with gene age. Older genes tend to be longer and carry a higher burden of deleterious de novo variants.
  • New genes with biomedical phenotypes have been integrated into the human genome at a remarkably steady pace, roughly 0.07% per million years.
  • Young genes are strongly enriched in diseases of the male reproductive system, and the X chromosome carries an excess of male reproductive disease genes, consistent with sexual selection and the "faster-X" and "faster-male" effects.
  • Young genes are also linked to phenotypes that may reflect human innovations, including brain size, intellectual development, musculoskeletal traits, and color vision.
  • Pleiotropy grows with gene age following a logistic curve. This led us to propose a "pleiotropy-barrier" model: because young genes have few functions, they face fewer constraints and have more room for phenotypic innovation than older, multifunctional genes.

Why are rare diseases rare?

Looking back, the answer to my original question may lie partly in the genes themselves. Rare disease genes tend to be old and conserved, and their conservation reflects strong negative selection. Harmful variants in these genes are removed efficiently from populations, which may be an important reason why rare diseases remain rare.

People behind the work

This work was a close collaboration with Dr. Manyuan Long. I am grateful to Patrick for laying the foundation of this project, to Manyuan for entrusting me with it and for the many discussions about new genes and evolutionary innovation, and to Bairong Shen for his long-term support from West China Hospital.
